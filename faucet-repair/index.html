Stuck valve or burst supply line: the urgent versions

Two situations turn a fixture problem into an emergency. The first is a shutoff valve that is frozen open, which leaves you unable to isolate a leak. The second is a braided supply line that has burst, which can empty a lot of water onto a cabinet floor in minutes.

What makes a faucet drip

Drips come from wear. In a compression faucet it is the rubber seat washer. In a cartridge faucet it is the cartridge or its O-rings. In a ball or ceramic disc design the seals or seats are worn. Mineral scale, which builds up faster in moderately hard water such as the 120 parts per million that Columbus reports as an average, accelerates the wear.

A small drip costs more than you expect. Many drips a minute add up to many gallons a month, and a leaking hot water faucet wastes energy on top of water. A repair now is cheaper than a ruined seat later.

Shutoff valves and supply lines

The small valves under sinks are often 30 or 40 years old in Hilliard's older homes, and they seize. A valve that has not moved in years can leak when turned. We replace it with a quarter-turn ball valve, which is more reliable and makes the next repair easier.

Supply lines last about as long as the faucet. If your braided lines are more than ten years old, replacing them when you replace the faucet is a sensible cost to add.

Repair or replace the faucet

A faucet that has dripped once can usually be repaired. If it has been repaired several times, the body is pitted or the finish is gone, replacement is better. We install what you supply or bring options, and see plumbing installation for fixtures in a remodel.

Burst supply line under a sink

Close the shutoff valves under the sink. If they will not close, shut the main. Empty the cabinet, mop the floor and photograph the damage before the plumber arrives, since insurance often covers sudden water.
